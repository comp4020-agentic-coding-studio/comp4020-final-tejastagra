# README.md outline (for Tejas to write)

Length: 400 to 600 words. Published in full at `/readme/`. Markers start here with only the URL, so the first lines must tell a newcomer how to get in. The course check `spec/invariants.test.ts` requires every `#` heading in README.md to appear, in order, in the HTML the server sends at `/readme/`, with no script running. So render it on the server, and if you change a heading, check `/readme/` still shows it.

This is a skeleton of questions, not text to copy. The course asks you to write this yourself.

## Suggested headings

1. **How to play** (two or three sentences, then the three ways in: computer, friend link, quick match). How does a marker try it alone, then with two windows?
2. **What good means here.** Your argument. Why simultaneous choices? Why does reading your opponent matter more than reading the board? Why crashes leave rubble and why one shield?
3. **Who it is for.** Two people, a phone each, a few minutes.
4. **What I chose not to build, and why.** Teams, chat, tournaments, spectating, liquid glass styling.
5. **Enforced and judged.** A small table: each promise, whether it is enforced by a check (name the check) or judged (say how: playtests in `docs/playtests.md`).
6. **What I read.** Your sources and what each one changed.

## Sources to start from (check each before citing)

- Robin Sloan, "An app can be a home-cooked meal" (2020): software for a handful of people.
- Clay Shirky, "Situated Software" (2004): software built for a specific group.
- David Sirlin on "yomi" (reading the mind of your opponent) in his writing on competitive game design.
- Victor Allis, *A Knowledge-based Approach of Connect-Four* (1988): standard Connect 4 is solved, which is one argument for changing the rules.
- Allan Calhamer's *Diplomacy* (1959): a classic simultaneous-move game.
- WCAG 2.2, success criteria 1.4.1 (Use of Color) and 2.5.8 (Target Size): the basis for the phone standards.
